# 03 - Interview preparation

Use this after a posting clears the shortlist and before the first call. The
goal is not a script. It is a bank of true stories you can reach for under
pressure, plus an honest plan for the parts of your history an interviewer will
probe.

---

## 1. Build the story bank first

Most UK interviews are competency-based: the interviewer has a scorecard of
criteria and marks your answers against it. You cannot improvise that. Build
6-8 stories that cover the criteria, then reuse them across questions.

### The STAR shape

| Element | What it must contain | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Situation** | The context in one or two sentences: company, team, constraint | Three minutes of background before the point |
| **Task** | What you specifically were responsible for | Describing the team's task, not yours |
| **Action** | What you did, in first person, in sequence, including the judgement call | "We decided", "it was decided" |
| **Result** | The outcome, with a number where one exists, plus what you learned | Trailing off without an outcome |

Aim for 90-120 seconds per story. Write each one on a card:

```text
STORY: <short name>
SITUATION: <where, when, constraint>
TASK: <what I owned>
ACTION: <3-5 steps, first person, including the decision I made>
RESULT: <number, and what changed afterwards>
REFLECTION: <what I would do differently>
COVERS: <competencies this story can answer>
```

The reflection line matters: UK interviewers often follow up with "what would
you do differently?" and a candidate who has not thought about it reads as
lacking self-awareness.

### Mapping stories to common UK competencies

| Competency | Story type to prepare | Typical question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delivering at pace | A deadline you protected by cutting scope | "Tell me about a time you delivered under pressure" |
| Stakeholder management | A disagreement with a senior stakeholder you resolved | "Tell me about managing a difficult stakeholder" |
| Influencing without authority | A change you drove across teams you did not manage | "Give an example of influencing others" |
| Problem solving | A diagnosis where the first hypothesis was wrong | "Tell me about a complex problem you solved" |
| Failure and learning | A real failure with a concrete fix afterwards | "Tell me about a time you failed" |
| Teamwork and conflict | A disagreement inside your team and how it ended | "How do you handle conflict?" |
| Prioritisation | Too much work, not enough time, and how you chose | "How do you prioritise competing demands?" |
| Customer focus | A decision changed by something a user said | "Tell me about improving something for a customer" |
| Leadership (if relevant) | Growing a person, not managing a project | "Tell me about developing someone" |
| Diversity and inclusion | A process you changed to include someone | Common in public sector and large employers |

Public sector and large-employer panels score each answer against published
criteria. Ask the recruiter for the criteria or the question areas in advance,
then map one story to each. "No" is a valid answer to "do you have the
questions?"; ask what the interview will cover instead.

## 2. Stage-specific preparation

### Stage 1: recruiter or talent screen (15-30 minutes)

What it is for: filtering on logistics and motivation, not deep skill.

Prepare concise answers to:

- **Why this role, why now?** One sentence on the role, one on what you want
  next. Nothing about "exciting opportunities".
- **Why are you leaving?** Neutral and forward-looking. Never criticise a
  current employer by name.
- **Salary expectations.** Give a range with a floor, based on the market check
  from `01-evaluation.md`. If asked for history, say: "I am targeting £X to £Y
  for this scope; my current package is comparable." Do not inflate; UK
  recruiters check.
- **Notice period.** Exact weeks, and whether you have any notice to work
  through.
- **Right to work.** State your status plainly if asked. If you need
  sponsorship, say so now: it saves everyone a wasted process.
- **Location and hybrid pattern.** Confirm what you can commit to.
- **Do you have any questions?** Always have two: what the process looks like
  and what success in the first six months looks like.

### Stage 2: hiring manager (45-60 minutes)

What it is for: can you do this job, at this level, with this team.

Prepare:

- Three stories that map to the advert's top three responsibilities.
- One question about the team's current constraint: "What is the hardest thing
  the team is dealing with right now?"
- One question about how performance is judged in the first year.
- A view on the product or service, from your own research. One specific
  observation, not flattery.

Do not pretend to know their domain if you do not. "I have not worked in
regulated insurance, but the closest thing I have done is X, and here is how I
would close the gap" is a strong answer.

### Stage 3: technical, task or case stage

What it is for: evidence of how you work, not whether you can be timed.

Before you start:

- Ask how long it should take, and how it will be assessed. If a "two hour"
  task would really take eight, say so and ask what to prioritise.
- Ask whether you may use your usual tools, documentation and AI assistants. If
  the answer is no, that is useful information about the team.
- Confirm the format: live coding, take-home, pair programming, presentation,
  or a case discussion.

During:

- Narrate your reasoning. Interviewers score the thinking, and silence is
  unscorable.
- Ask clarifying questions before writing code. Requirements ambiguity is part
  of the test.
- If you get stuck, say what you would look up and how. That is what the job
  actually looks like.
- For take-homes, write a short README: assumptions, trade-offs, what you would
  do with more time. It is usually read more closely than the code.
- For presentations, time it out loud, have one clear recommendation, and put
  the appendix behind it.

### Stage 4: panel or values interview

What it is for: consistency, values fit, and how you behave with several people.

Prepare:

- One story per panellist's area, not the same story four times.
- Expect a question on failure, one on inclusion, one on ambiguity.
- Expect "what would you do differently?" after every story. Have the answer.
- Note names and roles at the start; use them.
- If a panellist is silent, invite them in: "Did you want to probe the
  commercial side?" Panels notice candidates who manage the room.

### Stage 5: final or offer conversation

- Expect a conversation about start date, notice, and any relocation or hybrid
  arrangement.
- Do not negotiate before an offer exists. When it does, negotiate the package
  as a whole: base, bonus, pension, holiday, learning budget, start date.
- Get the offer in writing, including the IR35 status if it is a contract.

## 3. Handling gaps honestly

The rule: name it, bound it, move on. Interviewers forgive gaps that are
explained plainly. They do not forgive gaps that are hidden and then discovered.

### Redundancy

"The team was restructured in March and my role was one of nine removed. I had
already been running the reporting workstream, so I used the notice period to
finish that handover and then started looking deliberately rather than taking
the first thing available."

### Career break, caring or health

Give the reason at the level you are comfortable with, then the evidence that
you are current:

"I was out of work for 14 months caring for a parent. I kept my hand in with a
volunteer data project for a local charity, and I have been back in full-time
work since January."

You are not obliged to disclose a diagnosis or a disability. If you need an
adjustment, ask for it through the recruiter, which is a separate conversation
from the gap.

### No direct experience in the domain

"The closest thing I have done is X. The parts that transfer are A and B. The
part that is new is C, and my plan for the first month would be to shadow
someone doing it and read the internal documentation before touching anything."

Never claim adjacent experience as direct. It is the fastest way to lose a
technical interviewer's trust.

### Overqualified or coming from a bigger role

"I want to be doing the work rather than managing it, and this role is the level
where I do my best work. I am not looking for a title; I am looking for a team
building something I care about."

Have an answer ready for "won't you leave in six months?" It should be specific
to this role, not a promise.

### Short tenures or job hopping

Address the pattern once, factually, with the reason for each move, and end on
why this one is different. Do not blame every employer.

### Contracting and IR35 questions

Expect: "Why contract?", "Are you set up as a limited company?", "Is this role
inside or outside?" Answer plainly:

- Your working arrangement (limited company, umbrella, PAYE).
- Whether you have worked inside and outside IR35 before, and that you
  understand the difference.
- That you will work to the client's determination and the SDS, not to a verbal
  assurance.

Never advise the client on their IR35 determination, and never accept a role on
the basis of "we will call it outside". That is the client's call and their risk,
and a contractor who pushes for "outside" without a determination is a red flag
to a careful hirer.

### "Why should we hire you?"

One sentence of evidence, one of fit, stop. Not a summary of the CV.

## 4. Questions worth asking

Pick two or three per stage. Bad questions are the ones answered on the
company's home page.

Hiring manager:

- What does the team need to be true in six months for this hire to have been
  the right call?
- What is the hardest constraint on the team right now: people, time, or
  competing priorities?
- How does work get prioritised when two stakeholders disagree?
- What happened to the last person in this role?

Recruiter:

- What is the rest of the process, and who decides?
- Is the salary range fixed, or is there movement for the right candidate?
- Has this role been open long? (A long-open role often means an unrealistic
  spec.)

Peer or panel:

- What does a normal week look like, in hours and in meetings?
- What is the on-call or out-of-hours expectation, if any?
- What would you change about how the team works?

Questions to avoid: anything answered on the careers page, anything about
holiday or perks before an offer, and anything that implies you have not read
the advert.

## 5. Preparation timeline

**Three days before**

- Re-read the advert and the evaluation verdict. Note the three requirements
  they will test.
- Write or refresh the 6-8 story cards.
- Research the employer from primary sources: site, Companies House, recent
  dated news, the interviewer's public work if named.
- Prepare your two questions per stage.

**The day before**

- Rehearse two stories out loud, timed. Not in your head.
- Check the logistics: link, dial-in, room, parking, travel time, what to bring.
- Print or open the advert, the job description and your story cards.
- Decide what you will wear. For most UK tech and professional roles, smart
  casual is safe; for client-facing or public sector, a suit is still normal.

**On the day**

- Join or arrive ten minutes early, not twenty.
- Have water, a notepad and one page of notes: three stories, two questions,
  the employer's name and the interviewer's name.
- For video: check the camera at eye level, the light in front of you, and that
  your phone is silent. Do not read from a script on screen; it shows.

**After**

- Write down the questions asked and your answers while they are fresh. This is
  the only way the next interview gets easier.
- Send a short thank-you email within 24 hours: one paragraph, one specific
  reference to the conversation, no rehashing of the CV.
- If a recruiter gives a timeline, wait for it, then follow up once, politely.

---

## 6. If it goes badly

- A bad answer is not a failed interview. Ask to revisit it: "Can I come back to
  the stakeholder question? I gave you the wrong example."
- If you froze, say so and offer the answer in a follow-up email. It is better
  than leaving the panel with silence as the record.
- Ask for feedback either way, in one line, once. Many UK employers will not
  give detailed feedback for legal reasons; accept that and move on.
- Record what you would do differently, and update the story bank the same day.
